From Problem Descriptions to Decisions

LLM agents for optimization modeling

Language models now write optimization models from plain-language requests. When the answer matches a benchmark, what has been shown: that the request was complete, that the model is faithful to it, or only that the solver ran?

Yidi Miao · Heinz College, Carnegie Mellon University

01

The general framework

Most mechanisms combine a generator, a checker, and a use of the verdict.

The systems in this literature look different, but most combine the same three parts. A generator produces a chain of artifacts, from a specification to a solver result. A checker tests a stated relation on one of them, using some evidence. The verdict is then used in one of five ways. This is an analytical lens, not a claim that every system fits it. Reading a paper through it shows what its result can and cannot establish. Definitions, assumptions, and proofs are in Section 4 of the paper.

Repair guarantees only what its checker tests

Δ = (1 − p)·rI·γ − p·rC·δ

After one repair round, correctness changes by Δ. Here p is the share of correct models, rI and rC are the rates at which the checker rejects incorrect and correct ones, γ is the chance a repair fixes a wrong model, and δ the chance it breaks a correct one. A loop that ends with an acceptance has established only what its checker tests, such as executability; a loop that runs out of budget has established nothing. With the other rates fixed, the loss term grows as the generator improves.

More samples do not sharpen a weak filter

Pr(correct | accepted) = pα / (pα + (1 − p)β)

Given that some candidate is accepted, this is the chance that a uniformly chosen accepted one is correct, for independent samples. It does not depend on the number of samples; more samples only make it likelier that something is accepted. Overall success, counting abstentions as failures, is bounded by pass@k. Ranked selection, adaptive search, and correlated candidates are outside this statement. For an experience library, the poisoning rate is the complement, (1 − p)β / (pα + (1 − p)β).

02

Three correctness questions

One score hides three questions.

Most systems are judged by whether the optimal value of their model matches a reference answer. That number cannot tell a missing requirement from a wrong constraint or a solver error.

Q1

Does the description suffice?

“Meet demand at low cost” does not say whether shortages are forbidden or penalized. Both readings fit the text, and they give different plans when capacity is tight.

Q2

Is the formulation faithful?

A model can run, return an optimum, and still omit a requirement. Execution success and objective agreement are incomplete measures of fidelity.

Q3

Was it solved correctly?

Infeasibility may be a coding slip or a question for the user. Repairing until the solver is satisfied can change the problem being solved.

Decision sufficiency

A description is sufficient for a decision when every admissible reading shares an optimal action. Then missing details are harmless. Otherwise the agent should ask, or disclose the assumption it made. A benchmark that scores one reference value marks one of the readings wrong without saying why.

04

Evidence, with context

What the studies show, and what they do not.

Backbones, benchmark releases, and tolerances differ across papers, so the review compares mechanisms through within-study ablations and treats cross-paper numbers as illustrations. Five of the thirteen findings:

Evaluation reliability

A benchmark name does not identify a benchmark.

Reported NL4Opt test sets range from 213 to 289 instances, IndustryOR from 25 to 100, and success criteria from exact match to a 5% tolerance. Five groups have audited IndustryOR and curated it differently.

What this establishes. Numbers aggregated across papers cannot rank mechanisms. Each spread mixes backbones, releases, tolerances, and reproduction choices, and no study decomposes it.

Sources. Finding 1 and Table 9 in the paper. Chain-of-Experts endpoints: 28.0% in OptSkills (arXiv:2605.29829) and 75.0% in COOPA (arXiv:2606.27611). Audit share: Xiao et al. (arXiv:2508.10047).

Agent behavior

Execution repair buys executability more than correctness.

Repair loops turn crashes into models that run, and many of those remain wrong. Per additional executable model, the two studies shown had a net gain of 0.17 and 0.05 correct ones.

What this establishes. Studies of repair should report how many additional models are correct, not only how many run. Ratios are our computation from author-reported aggregate values, mostly single runs; they are net changes, not counts of individually repaired models.

Sources. Finding 6 in the paper. Text2Model (arXiv:2604.12955); ReLoop (arXiv:2602.15983).

Representations

Typed intermediates help; free-form ones can hurt.

Writing a typed scheduling language compiled to CP-SAT raised verified feasibility at a fixed backbone. A free-form four-stage reasoning template helped a frontier model but cut another model's execution rate.

What this establishes. In these comparisons, pipelines built around typed, compiled intermediates did better, and free-form plans changed sign across backbones. The comparisons change the representation together with other parts of the pipeline, so they do not isolate typing as the cause.

Sources. Finding 3 in the paper. Typed scheduling language (arXiv:2608.18409); reasoning template, ReLoop (arXiv:2602.15983).

21.7 → 55.3%verified feasibility: generic solver code vs. a typed scheduling language
91.1 → 53.2%execution rate of one model after adding a free-form reasoning template
Operational difficulty

Accuracy collapses with scale and realism.

On descriptions reverse-constructed from MIPLIB instances, average accuracy falls below one in five and nearly half of generated programs fail to run. With formulation structure held fixed, growing only the instance data is enough to break a small model.

What this establishes. High scores on self-contained word problems do not establish readiness for data-heavy or workspace tasks. Systems that scale keep structure compact and move data out of the prompt.

Sources. Finding 10 in the paper. MIPLIB-NL (arXiv:2602.10450); data scaling at fixed structure, GPT-5-Nano (arXiv:2605.21751).

Clarification and verification

No checker's guarantee is unconditional, and few are measured.

Checkers certify different relations and sit at very different points between accepting everything and rejecting everything. One test battery rejected none of 326 faithful models but let more than half of injected errors through; its soundness holds when the assertions extracted from the text are valid and each test's preconditions are met. LLM judges catch more at the price of false alarms. For clarification, one study's agent trained to ask reached 66.8% accuracy, against 0% when it could not ask and 80% with full information, with a simulated user.

What this establishes. Acceptance rates are reported for only a handful of checkers, under different error definitions, so the map is illustrative. The two clarification comparisons highlighted in the review, against a no-question or a one-turn baseline, both use LLM-simulated users, and neither isolates the effect of asking.

Sources. Findings 12 and 13 and Table 17 in the paper. Falsification tests and LLM-judge panel (arXiv:2607.16646); routing verifiers (arXiv:2403.10795); AutoOR (arXiv:2604.16804).

All thirteen findings
  1. Benchmark names do not identify benchmarks.
  2. Reference labels are noisy, and benchmark curation differs.
  3. Typed, machine-checked intermediates help; free-form intermediates can hurt implementation.
  4. Structured knowledge often helps, while retrieval and memory can also hurt.
  5. Combined supervised and solver-rewarded training helps in reported recipe comparisons.
  6. Execution repair buys executability more than correctness; LLM validation can also hurt.
  7. Scaffold benefits depend on backbone, task, and computation.
  8. Residual errors concentrate in constraints, variable definitions, and data binding.
  9. Multiple candidates expose selection headroom and remaining generation failures.
  10. Accuracy collapses with scale and realism, and the remedies move data and computation out of the prompt.
  11. Reported inference costs vary, and budgets are seldom matched.
  12. Two simulated-user comparisons report gains from richer interaction; the effect of asking is not isolated.
  13. Checkers disagree on what “equivalent” means and trade coverage for soundness.
05

Open problems

What the next systems need.

When is a description sufficient for a decision?

Benchmarks built around decision sufficiency could separate unjustified assumptions from formulation errors.

Asking the right question, and stopping

The value of a question is how much its answer can change the decision. Counting recovered facts weights every hidden fact equally, including the ones that do not matter.

Verifying against intent

Reference-based checks inherit reference errors, and reference-free checks verify the text rather than what the user meant. Verification can expose that several models fit; only information can choose among them.

Learning without answer keys

Rewards, data filters, and experience libraries mostly depend on reference values. In AdmitOR's label-blind stream, 241 of 878 executable candidates were wrong, about one in four (arXiv:2608.15565).
