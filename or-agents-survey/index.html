From Problem Descriptions to Decisions

LLM Agents for Optimization Modeling

Language models now write optimization models from plain-language requests. When the answer matches a benchmark, what has actually been shown: that the request was complete, that the model is faithful to it, or only that the solver ran?

Yidi Miao · Heinz College, Carnegie Mellon University

Read the working draft Companion results board Draft of 21 September 2026 · literature to 10 September 2026

Status: working draft, not peer reviewed. Screening and data charting were AI-assisted following a written PRISMA-ScR protocol. The author's verification of inclusions, sampled exclusions, and charted numbers is still in progress, and numbers are as reported by the cited authors.

01 / Perspective

One score hides three questions.

Most systems are judged by whether the optimal value of their model matches a reference answer. That number cannot tell a missing requirement from a wrong constraint or a solver error. The review organizes the literature around the three questions an agent has to answer between a request and a decision.

01

Does the description suffice?

“Meet demand at low cost” does not say whether shortages are forbidden or penalized. Both readings are consistent with the text, and they give different plans when capacity is tight.

02

Is the formulation faithful?

A model can run, return an optimum, and still omit a requirement. Execution success and objective agreement are incomplete measures of fidelity.

03

Was it solved correctly?

Infeasibility may be a coding slip or a question for the user. Repairing until the solver is satisfied can change the problem being solved.

02 / The lifecycle

Where the mechanisms sit.

Phases are activities, not an exclusive taxonomy: a study can contribute to several. The examples are representative, and the full list of included studies is in the paper's appendix.

Phase I

Information handling

  • Structuring the specification: NL4Opt, LLMOPT, R2C, TriVAL
  • Asking: OR-Clarify, SAGE-Agent, Ask–Clarify–Optimize, AutoOR
  • Grounding in files and data: OR-Space, ORPilot, LEAN-LLM-OPT, MIPLIB-NL
Phase II

Formulation: generate and check

  • Representations: IR2Solve, R2C, Text2Zinc, typed DSLs
  • Orchestration and search: CoE, OptiMUS, OR-LLM-Agent, OptiTree
  • Training: ORLM, OptMATH, SIRL, OR-R1
  • Checking: EquivaMap, ModelEquivBench, FLARE, falsification tests, AdmitOR
Phase III

Solving, repair, revision

  • Debugging loops and IIS-guided repair: ORLoopBench, OptiRepair
  • Reformulation for tractability: LinearizeLLM, FormuEvo, EvoCut
  • Revision and explanation: model patches, OptiGuide, OptiChat

Cross-cutting. Knowledge and experience libraries (AlphaOPT, OptSkills, Agora-Opt), benchmarks from word problems to workspaces, and the evaluation practices that decide what a score means.

03 / A formal view

Generators, checkers, and what you do with a verdict.

Most mechanisms combine three ingredients: something that generates artifacts, a checker that tests a stated relation, and a rule for using the checker's verdict. A checker accepts correct artifacts at rate α and incorrect ones at rate β. Simple identities then connect results that look unrelated. They are bookkeeping, not new theory: they say what a study has to report for its result to be interpretable.

Use of a verdictWhat happensExamples
RepairRejected artifacts are revised with the checker's evidence until it acceptsDebugging loops, IIS-guided repair, LLM validators
SelectionSample several candidates and return one the checker accepts or ranks highestBest-of-N, debate, tree search, confidence selection
AdmissionStore a lesson or skill only if the outcome is acceptedAlphaOPT, OptSkills, AdmitOR
RewardTrain the generator to maximize acceptance, or filter training data by itSIRL, OR-R1, OptMATH's rejection sampling
AskingQuery the user while the admissible readings lead to different decisionsInterOPT, SAGE-Agent, AutoOR
Repair

A loop guarantees only what its checker tests.

Δ = (1 − p)(1 − β)·γ − p(1 − α)·δ

γ is the chance a repair fixes a rejected wrong model, δ the chance it breaks a rejected correct one. With an execution checker the loop stops at “it runs”. With an imperfect LLM validator the second term grows as the generator improves.

Selection

More samples do not sharpen a weak filter.

Pr(correct | accepted) = pα / (pα + (1 − p)β)

The precision of filtering does not depend on the number of samples; k buys coverage. Pass@k is an oracle bound, and voting fails when wrong candidates share a misreading.

04 / Evidence, with context

What the studies show, and what they do not.

Backbones, benchmark releases, and tolerances differ across papers, so the review compares mechanisms through within-study ablations and flags cross-paper numbers as illustrations. Five of the thirteen findings:

Evaluation reliability

A benchmark name does not identify a benchmark.

Reported NL4Opt test sets range from 213 to 289 instances, IndustryOR from 25 to 100, and success criteria from exact match to a 5% tolerance. Five groups have audited IndustryOR and curated it differently.

47 ptsspread for Chain-of-Experts configurations on IndustryOR across papers
≥ 54%of IndustryOR instances flagged in one expert audit

What this establishes

Numbers aggregated across papers cannot rank mechanisms. The spread mixes backbones, releases, tolerances, and reproduction choices, and no study decomposes it.
Agent behavior

Execution repair buys executability more than correctness.

Adding execution validation to 1,318 MiniZinc generations produced 83 more executable models but 14 more correct ones. IIS-guided recovery raised one model's execution rate from 53.2% to 97.4% while strict accuracy moved from 3.7% to 5.8%.

0.17additional correct models per additional executable one (Text2Model)
0.05the same ratio for ReLoop's execution recovery

What this establishes

Studies of repair should report how many additional models are correct, not only how many run. Ratios are computed from author-reported counts, mostly single runs.
Representations

Typed intermediates help; free-form ones can hurt.

Writing a typed scheduling language compiled to CP-SAT raised verified feasibility from 21.7% to 55.3% at a fixed backbone. A free-form four-stage reasoning template helped a frontier model but dropped another model's execution rate from 91.1% to 53.2%.

What this establishes

The benefit of an intermediate depends on whether something deterministic implements it. Effects for free-form plans change sign across backbones, so single-backbone results do not transfer.
Operational difficulty

Accuracy collapses with scale and realism.

On descriptions reverse-constructed from MIPLIB instances, average Pass@1 falls to 17.85% and the execution-failure rate rises to 47.6%. With formulation structure held fixed, growing only the instance data lowered one model from 72% to 11%.

What this establishes

High scores on self-contained word problems do not establish readiness for data-heavy or workspace tasks. Systems that scale keep model structure compact and move data out of the prompt.
Clarification and verification

Asking helps in simulation; the effect of asking is not isolated.

On 100 LP descriptions with facts removed, a model that cannot ask scored 0%, an untrained two-turn protocol 18.5%, and an RL-trained one 66.8%, against 80% with full information. Checkers, meanwhile, certify different relations: one solver-based self-check is right 91.5% of the time when it passes but passes only 34.2% of correct answers.

What this establishes

Both outcome-scored clarification studies use LLM-simulated users, and neither tests the policy on complete inputs. No checker's guarantee is unconditional: each depends on a reference, extracted assertions, or a faithful formalization.
All thirteen findings
  1. Benchmark names do not identify benchmarks.
  2. Reference labels are noisy, and benchmark curation differs.
  3. Typed, machine-checked intermediates help; free-form intermediates can hurt implementation.
  4. Structured knowledge often helps, while retrieval and memory can also hurt.
  5. Combined supervised and solver-rewarded training helps in reported recipe comparisons.
  6. Execution repair buys executability more than correctness; LLM validation can also hurt.
  7. Scaffold benefits depend on backbone, task, and computation.
  8. Residual errors concentrate in constraints, variable definitions, and data binding.
  9. Multiple candidates expose selection headroom and remaining generation failures.
  10. Accuracy collapses with scale and realism, and the remedies move data and computation out of the prompt.
  11. Reported inference costs vary, and budgets are seldom matched.
  12. Two simulated-user comparisons report gains from richer interaction; the effect of asking is not isolated.
  13. Checkers disagree on what “equivalent” means and trade coverage for soundness.

05 / Open problems

What the next systems need.

When is a description sufficient for a decision?

A description is decision-sufficient when every admissible reading shares an optimal action. Benchmarks built around that notion could separate unjustified assumptions from formulation errors.

Asking the right question, and stopping

The value of a question is how much its answer can change the decision. Counting recovered facts weights every hidden fact equally, including the ones that do not matter.

Verifying against intent

Reference-based checks inherit reference errors, and reference-free checks verify the text rather than what the user meant. Verification can expose that several models fit; only information can choose among them.

Learning without answer keys

Rewards, data filters, and experience libraries mostly depend on reference values. Admitting every executable model into a library poisons about one entry in four.
